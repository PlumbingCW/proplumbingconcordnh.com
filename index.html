1.

What to do before the emergency plumber arrives.

When water is moving where it should not be, the first minute matters more than the first repair. Find the main shutoff valve, usually where the water line enters the basement near the meter, and turn it clockwise until it stops. Then open one cold tap at the lowest level of the house to let the pipes drain.

If water is near a light fixture, an outlet or the panel, stay out of that room and switch the breaker off only if you can reach it dry. For a backed-up toilet or floor drain, stop running water everywhere in the house. Our 24/7 emergency plumber can talk you through each step while a truck is on the way.

Smell gas? Do not call a plumber first.

Leave the building, then call 911 or your gas utility from outside. Do not flip switches or use a phone indoors. Once the utility says the line is safe, we can help with the plumbing side.

3.

How Concord water and winters shape the work.

Concord's drinking water starts at Penacook Lake and the Contoocook River and is treated with chloramine. The city reported 2022 hardness near 12 parts per million, which is soft by any standard. In practice that means very little scale inside water heaters and faucets, so we spend less time on lime buildup than plumbers in hard-water towns.

The weather does the damage instead. Frost in this part of New Hampshire reaches 48 inches, the minimum depth the city uses for footings, so a water service line buried shallower than that is a freeze risk. Unheated garages, crawlspaces and exterior walls on older houses are where we find most split pipes in a cold snap.

The city is not responsible for leaks on private property, so a leak inside your house or yard is yours to fix, and that is where we work. If you suspect a leak out by the street, Concord General Services takes those reports at 603-228-2737, with an after-hours line at 603-225-8696.

4.

Neighborhoods we serve inside Concord.

We take calls from every part of the city, including Downtown, the North End, the South End, the West End, East Concord, West Concord and Concord Heights, and out in Penacook, where the village has its own wastewater plant and a mill-era housing stock.

The city counts roughly 18,700 housing units. About half are single-family homes, and the rest are two-to-four unit buildings and larger apartment buildings. That mix shows up in our calls as everything from a single kitchen drain to a shared stack serving several apartments.
