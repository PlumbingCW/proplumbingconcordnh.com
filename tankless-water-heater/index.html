Tankless Water Heater in Concord, NH.

A tankless water heater heats water as it flows instead of storing it, so it can supply hot water as long as a faucet is open. In Concord we install, repair and flush tankless units, and we size them for cold New Hampshire winters when the incoming water is at its coldest.

1.

How tankless heaters work, and where they fall short.

When you open a hot tap, a flow sensor fires a gas burner or electric element that heats the water passing through a heat exchanger. There is no tank to lose heat when nobody is home, which is the source of the savings. The catch is flow. A unit can only heat so many gallons per minute by a set temperature rise.

In winter, groundwater is colder, so the same unit delivers less hot water than in summer. A heater that is fine in July may struggle in February when several fixtures run at once. Sizing for the worst case is the single most important decision.

3.

Flushing and descaling a tankless unit.

Minerals in the water build up inside the heat exchanger and narrow the passages. The result is lower flow, error codes, noise and shutdowns. Flushing with a descaling solution through isolation valves dissolves the scale and restores flow.

Concord's treated water is soft, with 2022 hardness near 12 parts per million, so scale builds slowly here. Homes on private wells in nearby towns, where hardness can be much higher, may need a flush more often. We tell you the interval that fits your water, instead of a generic yearly schedule.

Isolation valves make flushing quick. If your unit lacks them, we add them during the first service, which turns every later flush into a short visit.

4.

Tankless repair: the usual suspects.

Most tankless problems fall into a few groups. Error codes for ignition, flame or exhaust point to gas supply, venting or a dirty sensor. Cold-water sandwiches, where hot water briefly turns cold, come from low flow or a sensor issue. A unit that shuts off at low flow needs its minimum flow threshold checked, and sometimes an aerator cleaned.

If the heat exchanger has cracked or the unit is well past its life, replacement is cleaner than repair. A planned swap beats a no-hot-water emergency, though for a broken unit our repair team is on call 24/7.

5.

What it costs and whether it pays back.

Tankless units cost more to buy and more to install than a tank, because of gas line, venting and sometimes electrical upgrades. Savings come from not heating a stored tank, and for most households they accumulate slowly over many years. They make the most sense where space is tight, demand is steady or you plan to stay long term.

  • Gas line size. A tankless unit needs more gas volume than a tank, and a larger line adds cost.
  • Venting. Condensing units use plastic vent pipe, and runs through walls add labor.
  • Water quality. A water softener or scale filter in hard-water areas protects the heat exchanger.
  • Recirculation. A recirculation pump adds comfort at the farthest fixture and adds installation cost.
6.

Before you decide.

Ask yourself.

  • 1.How many fixtures run at the same time?
  • 2.Is there room for a larger gas line?
  • 3.Will the unit be outdoors, where freezing is a risk, or inside?
  • 4.Do I plan to stay in the house for ten years or more?
  • 5.Is my panel ready for an electric unit?

An exterior tankless unit needs freeze protection in New Hampshire. Most outdoor-rated models include built-in heaters, but a power outage during a cold snap can still be a risk, which we discuss before installing.
